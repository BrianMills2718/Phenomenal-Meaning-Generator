# Phenomenal Meaning Generator

## Core thesis

**Consciousness is the ontological substrate of meaning**: meaning exists fundamentally as a structure of conscious experience. Non-conscious entities such as words, symbols, measurements, or data possess meaning only derivatively through their relation to conscious contents.

The project is generative rather than merely definitional. Its aim is to show how increasingly complex meaning can emerge from primitive phenomenal contents through a small set of learning and structuring operations.

## Primitive phenomenal meaning

At the base level, a phenomenal content does not need to represent some further semantic object in order to be meaningful.

- The intrinsic meaning of a primitive conscious experience is its **phenomenal character**: what that experience is like.
- The conscious experience of red, for example, does not require an underlying proposition such as "red is electromagnetic radiation of a certain wavelength" to possess meaning.
- That scientific proposition is instead a much more complex semantic construction assembled from many already meaningful phenomenal and conceptual contents.

A useful formulation is:

> **The intrinsic meaning of a primitive phenomenal content consists in its phenomenal character; derivative and conceptual meanings arise from learned relations among phenomenal contents.**

## Phenomenal geometry

Phenomenal states appear to possess intrinsic similarity and difference relations.

Examples:

- moderate pain is closer to severe pain than severe pain is to pleasure;
- scarlet is closer to crimson than to blue;
- adjacent pitches are more similar than widely separated pitches.

This suggests a **phenomenal metric**: a structure in which conscious states occupy positions relative to one another.

Similarity need not therefore begin as a concept applied by a developed mind. It may instead be a structural fact about phenomenal states themselves.

For this structure to support learning, differences within phenomenal space must have consequences for subsequent processing: the learning system must be sensitive to phenomenal distance.

## Sensory grounding

The toy mind should begin with sensory experience rather than with pre-existing concepts.

Environmental and bodily processes produce structured phenomenal variation through:

- vision;
- audition;
- touch;
- proprioception;
- interoception;
- pain and pleasure;
- temperature;
- other sensory modalities.

Initially, the mind need not possess concepts such as *object*, *hand*, *mother*, *self*, *red*, or *cause*. It instead has a changing **multimodal phenomenal field**: simultaneous conscious variation across sensory modalities.

## Minimal generative machinery

A provisional minimal system contains:

1. **Phenomenal states**  
   At time (t), the mind occupies some conscious state (C_t).

2. **Phenomenal geometry**  
   States have intrinsic similarity and difference relations.

3. **Temporal succession**  
   The system experiences change: (C_t \rightarrow C_{t+1}).

4. **Memory**  
   Previous phenomenal states can leave persistent traces or later be partially reinstantiated.

5. **Attention / salience**  
   Some contents receive greater weighting than others.

6. **Association**  
   Repeated temporal proximity or co-occurrence strengthens relations among traces.

7. **Reactivation**  
   A present phenomenal state can partially reactivate associated prior states.

8. **Composition / binding**  
   Multiple contents can be integrated into a larger structured phenomenal whole.

The important methodological constraint is that higher-order capacities should be **derived** where possible rather than placed among the primitives. In particular, abstraction, categories, objects, symbols, propositions, and language should not be assumed at the start.

## Acquired meaning

A phenomenal state can acquire additional meaning through learned relations.

Its primitive meaning remains its own phenomenal character, but repeated experience can connect it to other phenomenal contents, expectations, actions, and transformations.

A provisional formulation is:

> **The acquired meaning of a phenomenal state consists in the structured field of other phenomenal states and transformations that it makes available through learned relations.**

This meaning need not initially be propositional. A current experience may "mean" something because it activates or anticipates other possible experiences.

## Developmental model: the toy infant

Infant development provides a useful model because it forces the theory to explain how a meaningful world can emerge from sensory experience without presupposing mature concepts.

### 1. Structured sensory variation

The infant has changing visual, auditory, tactile, proprioceptive, interoceptive, and affective experiences.

### 2. Temporal regularity

Some phenomenal states reliably precede or follow others.

Repeated sequences create learned expectations.

### 3. Sensorimotor contingencies

Actions and sensations covary systematically.

Examples:

- eye movement changes the visual field;
- arm movement changes proprioceptive experience;
- the same arm movement may shift a visible shape;
- contact between the arm and another surface creates tactile experience.

These regularities can generate meaning without requiring an explicit concept of action or body.

### 4. Cross-modal covariance

Patterns across multiple senses change together.

For example, one visual form may move whenever a particular proprioceptive sequence occurs and may generate tactile sensations when it contacts other surfaces.

Repeated covariance allows these contents to become integrated.

### 5. Proto-objects

Stable bundles of correlated phenomenal structure can become persistent learned units.

An object is therefore not assumed at the start. It emerges from regularities such as:

- feature co-occurrence;
- spatial continuity;
- temporal persistence;
- predictable transformation;
- cross-modal agreement.

### 6. Body and self

The infant's hand is a particularly useful test case.

A visual object:

- moves in close correspondence with motor output;
- covaries with proprioception;
- produces tactile consequences;
- remains unusually controllable relative to other visual objects.

From these regularities, a distinction between self-related and externally controlled structures may emerge.

### 7. Agents and recurring sources

Other stable multimodal structures can form around recurring sources such as a caregiver.

Long before the explicit concept *mother*, a rich phenomenal network can connect:

- face-like visual structure;
- voice;
- smell;
- warmth;
- touch;
- feeding;
- satiation;
- affective states.

### 8. Categories

If phenomenal similarity and associative learning are sufficient, repeated experiences can form **clusters**: groups of states treated as related because of their location in phenomenal space and their shared learned relations.

The theory should attempt to derive categories rather than assume them.

### 9. Symbols

Sounds or marks can become associated with already meaningful phenomenal structures.

A spoken token such as "mama" becomes meaningful because it is attached to an existing network of phenomenal relations.

Language therefore does not create meaning from nothing. It attaches reusable symbols to previously structured meanings and permits those structures to be communicated and recombined.

### 10. Composition and recursion

Once learned meanings can themselves become components of further conscious structures, increasingly complex concepts can emerge.

Previously constructed meanings become objects of thought, allowing:

- abstraction;
- compositional concepts;
- propositions;
- theoretical entities;
- mathematics;
- scientific concepts;
- reflection on meaning itself.

## Example: red

A primitive red experience has intrinsic phenomenal meaning simply by being the experience it is.

Subsequently:

1. similar red experiences form a learned cluster;
2. a category corresponding to redness can emerge;
3. linguistic tokens such as "red" become associated with that category;
4. learned concepts such as light, wavelength, measurement, electromagnetic radiation, and numerical intervals become interconnected;
5. the proposition "red corresponds to a range of electromagnetic wavelengths" becomes possible.

The scientific proposition is therefore **not the hidden meaning of the primitive red experience**. It is a recursively constructed semantic network ultimately grounded in phenomenal contents.

## Generative thesis

The central hypothesis is:

> **Given phenomenally meaningful primitives plus sufficiently powerful operations for association, discrimination, memory, composition, abstraction, and recursion, complex semantic structures can be generated without introducing a fundamentally non-phenomenal source of meaning.**

The research program is to determine the **minimal** set of operations required and to demonstrate, stage by stage, that each higher semantic capacity can emerge without silently presupposing the very meaning it is intended to explain.

## Metaphysical tangent: phenomenal causal efficacy

A related but separable issue arose concerning **phenomenal causal efficacy** (the capacity of differences in conscious experience to make a causal difference to subsequent events).

The meaning-generator itself need not initially solve the metaphysics of mind. It requires only an operational assumption:

> **Phenomenal differences can affect learning.**

If the phenomenal similarity between two states is to help generate categories or associations, the system must somehow be sensitive to that similarity.

Several metaphysical interpretations remain open:

- **epiphenomenalism**: physical processes cause conscious states, but conscious states exert no causal influence;
- **identity or monist theories**: phenomenal and physical descriptions refer to the same underlying causal process;
- **panpsychist approaches**: phenomenal properties are fundamental and become integrated into more complex conscious systems;
- **phenomenal causal efficacy**: phenomenal differences themselves participate in causal pathways.

The current project can bracket these alternatives while developing the toy model.

## Panpsychist intuition

A tentative background picture considered during reconstruction was:

- consciousness may be fundamental rather than created from non-conscious matter;
- extremely simple physical systems might correspond to extremely simple or static phenomenal states;
- biological evolution may integrate these primitive phenomenal structures into increasingly complex consciousness;
- the **combination problem**—how simple phenomenal subjects or properties could compose a unified subject—remains unresolved.

An evolutionary puzzle arises if structured consciousness is entirely causally inert: why should evolved physical systems produce phenomenology that is coherent, integrated, and systematically tracks behaviorally relevant external structure?

This issue is not required for the generative theory of meaning, but it may later constrain the metaphysics in which the theory is embedded.

## Immediate research program

The next phase should construct an explicit **toy mind** and test whether semantic structure genuinely emerges.

The key methodological rule is:

> **Do not give the toy mind a concept unless the previous machinery has earned it.**

A promising starting problem is the development of the infant's representation of its own hand, because it requires the model to derive:

- temporal association;
- prediction;
- multimodal binding;
- sensorimotor contingency;
- object persistence;
- agency;
- self/other differentiation.

If these can be generated from primitive phenomenal structure plus minimal learning operations, the same machinery can then be extended toward categories, language, abstract concepts, and propositional thought.
